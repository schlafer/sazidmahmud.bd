When Excellence Becomes Obsession

Jul 4, 2026 · 12 min · 2545 words · Sazid Mahmud

The Illusion of Being a Fraud#

Feeling like a fraud is, above all, a matter of perception. It is about how you imagine others interpret your abilities, your intentions, and your worth. You begin to believe that every action is being observed, every word evaluated, every decision quietly measured against some invisible standard. You assume that people are searching for flaws, waiting for the moment you make a mistake.

That mistake becomes terrifying because, in your mind, it does more than reveal an error. It reveals you. It confirms the suspicion you have carried all along—that you never truly belonged, that your achievements were accidental, and that sooner or later everyone will discover the truth. You fear that once the illusion is broken, everything you have built will come crashing down.

Yet this drama unfolds almost entirely within the confines of your own mind. Rarely do people accuse you of being a fraud. Rarely do they scrutinize your every movement with the intensity you imagine. There may have been isolated moments of criticism or failure that planted the seed of doubt, but isolated moments are not reliable evidence of a permanent condition. If, on the other hand, your belief stems from sustained abuse or humiliation, then the issue is no longer merely impostor syndrome—it is trauma, and trauma is a different matter altogether.

If your struggle is simply the persistent conviction that you are inadequate, then your mind may be transforming a small uncertainty into an overwhelming catastrophe. It mistakes a passing shadow for a permanent darkness.

Ironically, the roots of these feelings are not entirely destructive. They emerge from qualities that are, in moderation, deeply valuable.

Your introspection allows you to recognize your weaknesses and refine your strengths. Your self-awareness helps you understand your place among others, making empathy and cooperation possible. Your willingness to question yourself prevents arrogance and dogmatism. It keeps you open to learning rather than imprisoned by certainty.

These are virtues.

The danger lies not in possessing them, but in allowing them to grow without restraint. Introspection becomes rumination. Self-awareness becomes self-absorption. Healthy skepticism becomes relentless self-condemnation. Every strength, when taken beyond its proper measure, transforms into its opposite.

This is true of almost everything in life. The distinction between medicine and poison is rarely one of substance; it is one of dosage.

Food illustrates this perfectly. Too little nourishment leads to starvation. Too much can destroy the body. Only the appropriate amount sustains life.

The mind operates according to the same principle.

A complete absence of self-reflection produces arrogance, recklessness, and an inability to learn from mistakes. A person who never questions themselves becomes blind to their own limitations. But excessive introspection disconnects you from reality, trapping you inside an endless conversation with yourself.

Too little self-consciousness leaves you oblivious to the impact you have on others. Too much makes every interaction revolve around your imagined inadequacies, convincing you that everyone is paying far more attention to you than they actually are.

Too little self-doubt breeds overconfidence. Too much erodes your ability to act at all. Every decision becomes paralyzing because no choice can ever feel sufficiently certain.

The goal, then, is not to eliminate self-doubt, introspection, or self-awareness. These are indispensable parts of a healthy mind. The goal is to keep them within their proper bounds—to cultivate enough humility to continue learning, enough confidence to keep acting, and enough perspective to recognize that your inner critic is not always an accurate witness to reality.

When Perfectionism Becomes Self-Defeat#

There is a concept from ancient Greek thought that deserves more attention than it usually receives: hubris. While many associate the word with arrogance or excessive pride, its deeper meaning is broader. Hubris describes any human tendency that exceeds its proper limits. Any virtue pushed so far that it ceases to be virtuous.

In Greek mythology and tragedy, hubris invited the wrath of the gods. Those who believed themselves beyond measure were eventually brought back to earth through suffering. Whether or not one accepts the religious worldview behind these stories is beside the point. Their enduring lesson is that excess has consequences. Just as medicine heals in the proper dose but poisons in excess, so too can our psychological strengths become liabilities when left unchecked.

This idea is particularly relevant to those who struggle with impostor syndrome. The anxiety they experience is often not rooted in a lack of ability, but in an excess of otherwise admirable qualities—most notably perfectionism and conscientiousness.

To be conscientious is to care deeply about your work. To be a perfectionist is to hold yourself to high standards and strive for excellence. These traits are not flaws. Alongside introspection, self-awareness, and healthy self-criticism, they are among the qualities that allow people to grow, improve, and contribute meaningfully. Without them, mediocrity becomes comfortable and progress becomes accidental.

The problem arises when excellence ceases to be an aspiration and becomes an obsession.

Once perfectionism crosses that threshold, it no longer motivates achievement; it prevents it. You begin a project with genuine enthusiasm and ambitious goals. You devote yourself to the work, convinced that enough effort will produce something flawless. Yet as the project develops, imperfections inevitably appear. Instead of accepting them as part of the creative process, you interpret them as evidence that the work has failed.

So you revise.

Then you revise again.

The deadline moves further away. Every iteration feels insufficient. Every improvement only sharpens your awareness of what remains unfinished. What began as a commitment to quality slowly transforms into paralysis. You become trapped in an endless cycle of refinement, convinced that what you have created still falls short of the impossible standard you imagined at the outset.

Eventually, the problem ceases to be the project itself.

It becomes your opinion of yourself.

Rather than recognizing that your expectations were unrealistic, you conclude that you are simply incapable. You mistake excessive standards for personal inadequacy. Instead of questioning your perfectionism, you question your competence.

This is where perfectionism gives way to defeatism.

You begin telling yourself that no amount of effort will ever be enough. Failure no longer appears as a possibility among many; it becomes your presumed destiny. Every setback confirms what you already believe—that you were never suited for the task in the first place.

Defeatism, however, rarely stops there.

Over time, it hardens into something even more corrosive: a form of nihilistic self-deprecation. You stop believing merely that your work is flawed. You begin believing that it is meaningless. That nothing you create has value. That every effort is ultimately wasted.

This progression from conscientiousness to perfectionism, from perfectionism to defeatism, from defeatism to self-contempt—is not the result of reality. It is the result of hubris. Not the hubris of arrogance, but the hubris of demanding from yourself what no human being can consistently deliver.

The remedy is not to abandon ambition or lower your standards until they no longer challenge you. It is to restore proportion.

Aim for excellence, but allow room for imperfection. Care deeply about your work, but do not make your worth dependent upon its flawlessness. Hold yourself accountable, but not hostage.

Balance is not the enemy of greatness. It is the condition that makes greatness sustainable.

Even If You Were a Fraud#

Impostor syndrome is not merely an uncomfortable feeling. Left unchecked, it can erode your confidence, diminish your productivity, and gradually undermine your peace of mind. It deserves to be taken seriously. Yet overcoming it does not always require silencing the fear. Sometimes it requires confronting it directly.

One useful exercise is to stop arguing against the accusation and instead follow it to its logical conclusion.

Suppose you really are a fraud.

What, exactly, happens next?

Most people spend their energy trying to prove to themselves that they are not impostors. But this often strengthens the fear, because every accomplishment becomes another piece of evidence that must be defended. A different approach is to ask a more unsettling question:

What if the accusation were true?

Imagine you are presenting your work, writing code, publishing an article, or giving a public talk. Someone watches and concludes that you are not as knowledgeable or competent as you appear. Does that thought, existing entirely within another person’s mind, suddenly erase the value of what you are doing? Does it rob you of your curiosity, your enjoyment, or your ability to contribute?

Of course not.

The work remains exactly as it was before. The only thing that has changed is someone’s opinion—or perhaps only your assumption about their opinion.

Now push the thought experiment even further.

Imagine that, in some profound sense, you discover that you really are a fraud. You were mistaken about yourself all along.

What changes?

Does the world suddenly stop turning? Do your previous achievements disappear? Does everyone who learned something from your work instantly lose that knowledge? Does every meaningful conversation become meaningless?

Hardly.

Perhaps your self-image changes, but reality does not rearrange itself to accommodate that thought. The world continues much as it did before. Your internal narrative shifts; external reality remains remarkably indifferent.

This becomes even clearer when we consider roles that carry genuine responsibility.

Suppose you are a physician. Lives depend on your competence. If you privately believe you are a fraud, does that belief make everyone else safer? Quite the opposite. The fact that you occupy such a position means that you have already met demanding standards, undergone rigorous training, and demonstrated abilities that most people simply do not possess. Your self-doubt is not evidence of incompetence. If anything, it often reflects an acute awareness of the complexity of your profession.

The same reasoning applies, albeit on a smaller scale, to less consequential pursuits.

Perhaps you develop software, write essays, create art, or share ideas online. Someone may think your work is unimpressive. Someone else may find it genuinely useful. Neither opinion changes the intrinsic value of your effort. If your code solves a problem, it solves the problem regardless of whether its author feels like an expert. If your writing helps one reader think more clearly, it has already justified its existence.

Your worth is not determined by the certainty with which you hold it.

This is where an interesting reversal becomes possible.

The same nihilistic voice that once whispered, “Nothing I do matters,” can be turned against itself.

If nothing ultimately matters in the catastrophic way your anxious mind insists, then neither does the terrifying possibility of being exposed as a fraud. If the universe is not keeping score of every imperfection, then why should you? If one mistaken judgment from another person does not fundamentally alter reality, then why allow it to dictate your actions?

Used destructively, nihilism convinces you that your efforts are pointless.

Used constructively, it reveals how absurd it is to let imagined judgments imprison you.

The goal is not to become indifferent to your work or careless about its quality. It is to recognize that your fear of being “found out” rests on an illusion of enormous consequences. In reality, most of those consequences exist only in your imagination.

So ask yourself the question your anxiety desperately wants you to avoid:

If I really were a fraud, what would actually happen?

More often than not, the honest answer is this:

Far less than you think. And certainly not enough to justify sacrificing your curiosity, your creativity, or your willingness to keep contributing.

The Ideal as Compass, Not Judge#

Another way to loosen the grip of impostor syndrome is to rethink the relationship between the ideal and the actual.

As we have seen, impostor syndrome often grows out of perfectionism or conscientiousness pushed beyond their proper limits. The problem is not that we aspire to excellence. The problem is that we misunderstand what excellence is meant to do.

The ideal is supposed to guide us.

Instead, we often use it to condemn ourselves.

An ideal exists to provide direction. It gives us something to strive toward, something that inspires us to improve. Every meaningful achievement begins with an image of something better than what currently exists. Without ideals, there would be little reason to learn, create, or grow.

But an ideal is not a destination that can be fully reached.

It is, by its very nature, an abstraction. It represents perfection stripped of the limitations imposed by reality. Limitations such as time, experience, energy, uncertainty, and the countless circumstances that shape every human endeavor. Because the ideal exists only in thought, it can never be perfectly realized in practice.

What we can achieve is not perfection, but approximation.

The mistake is to compare reality to the ideal only to dismiss reality as inadequate. If every actual accomplishment is judged solely by how far it falls short of perfection, then nothing will ever be considered good enough. Every success becomes a failure simply because it is not flawless.

In that way, the ideal ceases to inspire.

It begins to sabotage.

A simple thought experiment illustrates the point.

Imagine two versions of yourself existing side by side.

One is the person you are today.

The other is the person you hope to become years from now—more knowledgeable, more experienced, more accomplished. The future version has written the books you dream of writing, built the projects you wish to build, mastered the skills you are only beginning to learn.

Now imagine your younger self looking at that future version with admiration.

Perhaps they think:

I would love to create work like that one day. Maybe I should begin writing, building, or sharing my ideas. But who am I kidding? I have nothing worth saying. Look at everything they have already accomplished. Compared to them, I’m hopelessly unqualified.

If that younger self believes those thoughts, what happens?

Nothing.

No book is written.

No skill is developed.

No experience is gained.

The very person they admire can never come into existence because they refuse to take the first imperfect step toward becoming them.

The comparison destroys the possibility of growth.

The irony is that the accomplished version of yourself exists precisely because they once tolerated being inexperienced. They accepted producing work that was imperfect. They allowed themselves to learn publicly, to make mistakes, to improve gradually.

Every expert was once a beginner whose work failed to resemble the ideal.

The ideal did not mock them.

It guided them.

This is the relationship we ought to cultivate with our aspirations.

Look toward those who inspire you—not to measure your inadequacy, but to illuminate your direction. Let their achievements remind you of what is possible, not of what you supposedly lack. Their present is not the standard by which your beginning should be judged.

The ideal is a compass, not a courtroom.

Its purpose is to point you toward better possibilities, not to sentence your present efforts as worthless. If every comparison leaves you discouraged rather than motivated, then you are using the ideal against yourself.

Growth does not require perfection.

It requires movement.

So stop asking whether your work resembles the ideal.

Ask instead whether it is better than yesterday’s.

That is enough.

If the good continues becoming better, then the ideal has already fulfilled its purpose. Not as your enemy, but as your guide through the uncertainty of becoming.
